# Part 2: Governance Lessons Learned

The strongest lesson from this analysis is that governance failures rarely occur in isolation; they compound. The simulated attack succeeded not because of one missing control, but because four separate layers were all absent at once: the patch wasn't applied, the database credentials were never changed, the network wasn't segmented, and nothing was watching for the attempt. Any single one of those controls, if enforced, could likely have stopped or contained the breach. This mirrors Equifax's own experience, where the Apache Struts vulnerability was only one piece of a wider breakdown involving unclear ownership, unenforced policy and absent detection.

The second lesson is that policy existing on paper is not the same as policy being effective. The simulated environment's governance tracker recorded a Patch Management Policy and a Vulnerability Management Policy before the attack ever ran, yet neither stopped the Struts flaw from remaining exploitable. This is the clearest parallel to Equifax: having a policy is a governance input, not a governance outcome. What determines whether a breach happens is whether that policy is actually enforced, measured, and acted on, which the simulation's three "Red" status metrics (patch compliance, remediation time, incident count) make visible in a way a policy document alone cannot.
